## Goal and Function of Visuals

- Infographics serve as a **content marketing tool**: drive website traffic, build brand awareness, improve search engine ranking (p.105)
- Visuals are remembered better than text: the **Picture Superiority Effect** means visualized information is much more likely to be recalled days later (p.136)
- A recruiter or hiring manager looks at an individual resume for an average of **six seconds**; infographic resumes communicate key information faster than text in that window (p.136)
- Infographic resumes must "tell a story" about the candidate — background, history, education, skills, experience, achievements (p.137)
- The key distinction of infographic resumes: graphic elements must **visualize data or information**, not merely decorate — they must communicate more effectively than text (p.132)

## Design Process Guidance

### Online Lifespan as a Design Constraint
- Topic and data selection determine how long a visualization stays relevant (p.119)
- Match infographic lifespan to goal: SEO traffic → choose evergreen topics; brand awareness → trending topics acceptable (p.119)
- Breaking-news infographics may have a lifespan of only **one week** (Bin Laden compound example, p.120); annual budget data → one year; historical or food-pairing data → many years (p.120–121)
- **Rule of thumb**: the most recent views and links are more heavily weighted by search engines — old viral content provides diminishing returns (p.119)

### Infographic Release Strategy (3-step)
1. **Landing page** — dedicate a single URL; short, keyword-rich URL with hyphens (not underscores); content: title tag, description text, alt text, social sharing buttons, embed code (p.122–127)
2. **Self-promotion** — distribute via all company communication channels; post to social media multiple times over several days, each time highlighting a different piece of data (p.127–129)
3. **Outreach** — contact topic-specific blogs and news media; evaluate blogs on: active status, PageRank, traffic, followers, whether they have posted infographics before (p.129–130)

### Infographic Resume Design Process
- Identify the **3 or fewer** most important aspects of a candidate's history for the target role — best designs limit visualizations to this number (p.137)
- Choose what to highlight: timeline of history, relative skill proficiencies, geographic locations (p.137)
- Infographic resume must complement, not replace, a traditional text resume — attracts attention and highlights key strengths; the full resume supplies detail (p.133)

## Common Mistakes and Anti-Patterns

- Too many data visualizations = visual noise, reader confusion — best designs use **3 or fewer** charts (p.137)
- Failing to dedicate marketing effort after publishing; publishing and hoping audiences find it organically is ineffective (p.122)
- Using an infographic topic unrelated to the company's business (link bait problem): search engines cannot determine if links are genuine endorsements (p.113–115)
- Infographics are just images to search engines — without text on the landing page, search spiders have no content to index (p.125)
- Stuffing alt text or embed code with a long string of keywords is spammy and counterproductive (p.126, p.128)
- Résumé: using an inappropriate design tone (e.g., lighthearted for a serious role); not printing correctly on standard paper sizes; highlighting wrong attributes for the desired position (p.136)
- Showing no numeric labels on relative skill visualizations is acceptable — values are subjective and their absence keeps the design honest (p.138, p.139)

## Practical Rules of Thumb

- Social media **half-life of a posted link** is ~3 hours (Facebook/Twitter); post the same infographic multiple times across several days, spacing posts at least 5 hours apart (p.129)
- YouTube links have a much longer half-life (~7.4 hours) — not directly applicable to static infographics (p.129)
- Sharing buttons should appear **above the fold** on the landing page (p.126)
- Keep the landing page URL short, use hyphens as word separators (p.124)
- For geographic maps in resumes: show only the map area needed — do not show an entire world map if the candidate only worked in one country (p.141)
- Overlapping areas on a career timeline are better than sequential blocks — they honestly convey concurrent roles and dual activities (p.138)
- Area charts are the **most common** visualization for career timelines; line charts, number lines, and overlapping shapes are also used (p.139)
- Bar charts are the most common for skill comparison; alternatives: circle sizes, sliders, bubble charts, Sankey diagrams, rose/radar diagrams (p.141)

## Metaphor-Based Design

- **"Climbing a mountain"** metaphor for Google PageRank: x-axis = PageRank scale 0–10, diagonal line from lower left to upper right represents "level of effort," real websites labeled at their rank position along the slope (p.105)
- Michael Anderson's resume uses **"area represents relative energy expenditure over time"** — a layered area chart where the subjective variable is created by the designer, not measured (p.132)
- Randall Knapp's resume labels his area chart **"energy expenditure over time"** and separately **"work produced over time"** — two different semantic interpretations of the same chart form (p.138)
