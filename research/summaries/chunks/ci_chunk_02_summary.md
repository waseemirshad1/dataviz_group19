# [agent_02] Cool Infographics — pages 51-100

## Overview of pages covered

Pages 51–65 conclude Chapter 1 (animated and interactive infographics) and pages 66–100 cover Chapter 2 (Online Infographics), including layout decisions, types of online infographics, and legal/copyright considerations.

---

## Goal and Function of Visuals

- Infographics translate large, complex datasets into visual stories that the general audience can quickly understand and share (p.66)
- Online infographics are "a complete story or article designed into a single image" combining text, images, illustrations, and data visualizations (p.66)
- Visuals help drive web traffic and engagement; they are often more effective than images alone (p.66)
- The amount of data to filter through grows constantly; infographic designs are "one of the best tools we have to share and communicate the things we can learn from that information" (p.65, Final Thoughts)
- Data visualizations imply scientific, quantitative credibility that appears objective — even if the designer made subjective choices about what to include (p.84)

## Interaction Design

- **Interactive infographics** give readers some control over the data or visualization displayed, keeping engagement longer than static infographics (p.58)
- Key interaction patterns shown in these pages:
  - **Zoom in/out** — NYT 2012 election map allowed zooming to states and counties, changing numeric display (p.58)
  - **Filter by category** — Tableau's "Tale of 100 Entrepreneurs" allowed selecting by industry segment (checkboxes); updated both chart and data table simultaneously (p.59)
  - **Slider/temporal filter** — "Inside Super PACs" used a month slider to change which PACs appeared in a bar chart (p.60)
  - **Click to highlight** — pie charts in the Super PACs infographic were clickable to drill into spending flows (p.60)
  - **Real-time data update** — NYT election map updated as results came in (p.58)
- Interactive infographics are often standalone data visualizations or built into larger infographic designs (p.58)
- Tableau Public is a free tool for creating interactive charts that can be embedded in websites (p.59)

## Layout and Format Decisions

- **Tall / vertical format** is the dominant online format: readers scroll down, not sideways; browsers fit width not height (p.67–68)
- Rule of thumb: publish at **800 pixels wide** and as long as needed; this fits most browser windows (p.68)
- When shared/reposted, images shrink to fit blog content widths (~600px); tall format remains readable, horizontal format becomes unreadably tiny (p.68–69)
- Tall format infographics scale down to 500px wide and remain somewhat legible; horizontal formats become 100px tall — useless (p.69)
- **Width is the size-limiting factor** when posting infographics online (p.69, NOTE box)
- Pinterest's vertical scrolling interface is ideally suited to tall format designs (p.70)
- Tall formats designed for online do not print well on standard A4/letter-size paper — scale down to be too small to read (p.71)
- If printing is a goal: design to fit a single page, or design in sections that can be printed separately (p.72)

## Types of Infographics and Their Design Purposes

### Informative Infographics (p.72–78)
- Present genuinely useful information without overt sales pitch
- More likely to be shared than advertisements
- Establish brand authority by demonstrating knowledge
- Example: "The Common Cook's How-Many Guide to Kitchen Conversions" — pure information, no sales pitch, went viral and sold as a printed poster (p.76–77)
- Example: "The Lifespan of Storage Media" (CrashPlan) — compared storage reliability across media types using horizontal bar charts; established credibility for cloud backup without directly selling it (p.78–79)

### Persuasive Infographics (p.80–86)
- Lead readers to a predetermined conclusion and then provide a call to action
- Structured narrative: Key message → Problem → Danger → Solution → What can I do? (Monroe's Motivated Sequence) (p.85–86)
- Data visualizations in persuasive designs imply objectivity even when the designer selected data to make a point (p.84)
- Readers often don't question infographic data as they would other sources — "learned it from an infographic, so it must be true" (p.84)
- Example: "Most Polluted Cities 2012" (American Lung Association) — built credibility then ended with three clear calls to action (p.83)
- Example: "Can Soap Make You Sick?" (GOJO Industries) — structured as: Key message / What's the problem? / What's the danger? / What's the solution? / What can I do? (p.85–86)

### Visual Explanations (p.87–89)
- Explain ideas, processes, or relationships — not statistics
- Use illustrations, diagrams, and icons (occasionally data visualizations)
- Example: "How Our Laws Are Made" — step-by-step process diagram with no charts or graphs, only illustrations and text (p.87)
- Example: "How Affiliate Marketing Works" — 10-step process on a path with color-coded character glyphs showing each actor's role (p.88–89)
- Visual explanations establish company authority; readers often print/keep these for future reference (p.87)

### Infographic Advertisements (p.89–93)
- Persuasive infographics with an explicit call to purchase or try a product
- Less likely to be shared than purely informative infographics (p.89)
- Better for quality of readers (targeted customers) than raw traffic volume (p.89, NOTE box)
- Use cases: comparing competitive products, assembly/usage directions, product benefits/specifications, consulting processes (p.89)
- Example: P.F. Chang's menu pairing infographic — dish photos paired with ingredient icon glyphs (p.90–92)
- Example: "Making an Organic Choice" (SoNice) — side-by-side comparison of conventional vs. organic farming across 6 dimensions (p.93–94)

### PR Infographics (p.94–98)
- Supplement press releases; target journalists and news media
- People trust news reports more than advertisements (p.94)
- Example: Honda Accord 30 Years — a timeline/comparison table of 9 car generations with multiple data columns per row (p.95–96)
- Example: Hotels.com Bi-Annual Price Index — combined maps, bar charts, and city skyline illustrations (p.97)

### Infographic Posters (p.98–100)
- Niche market of designer-selected topics, sold as wall posters
- Examples: HistoryShots (Genealogy of Pop/Rock Music, American Presidency), Timeplots, PopChartLab (Insanely Great History of Apple) (p.98–100)

## Animation and Video Infographics

- Animated GIF format: self-contained animation that functions when posted on other sites — advantage over code-based animations (p.51)
- Example: Cheetah infographic (Jacob O'Neal) — animated GIF showing running motion integrated into a static information design (p.51)
- Animated webpage infographics: "How Far is it to Mars?" — scrolling triggers animation; Earth shown at 100 pixels wide, user scrolls through vast empty space before Mars appears, conveying true scale (p.52–53)
- Video infographics: Royksopp "Remind Me" (2007) — data visualizations integrated into music video showing data about everyday life (circulatory system, commuter transport as pictograms, crowds color-coded by employment type) (p.54–55)
- "Did You Know? Shift Happens" — viral PowerPoint-to-video conversion; demonstrated that animated slide transitions + data = compelling video infographic (p.56–57)
- Tools: PowerPoint, Keynote, Prezi can be exported as video files for distribution (p.57)

## Common Mistakes and Anti-Patterns

- Horizontal format infographics fail when shared online — reduced to unreadably small sizes (p.69)
- Horizontal layouts cannot scroll sideways on most sites; width reduction of 87.5% results in 98.4% area reduction (p.69)
- "Our Amazing Planet" tall format — great online, but printed at poster size still must be significantly reduced (p.71–72)
- Persuasive designs risk making readers skeptical if the agenda is too obvious (p.81)
- Readers should check sources but often don't — infographics carry false authority (p.84)
- Finding images via Google image search for use in infographics = copyright infringement risk (p.100)

## Practical Rules of Thumb

- Tall format: publish at 800px wide; design as long as needed (p.68)
- When printing is a goal alongside online, consider designing in sections (p.72)
- Informative infographics outperform advertisements for traffic and sharing (p.72–73)
- For persuasive infographics, structure as: problem → danger → solution → call to action (p.85–86)
- All data visualizations have some bias — designers choose what to include (p.84)
- Always include copyright notice (©) in the infographic itself; even without it, automatic protection still applies (p.99, NOTE box)
- Recommended license for infographics: Creative Commons CC-BY-NC-ND (p.99)
- Use royalty-free stock images (iStockPhoto, Shutterstock) rather than Google image search (p.100)
