## Goal and Function of Visuals

- Infographics serve as **advertisements for a person or organisation** — their job is to attract attention and make information memorable, not to replace detailed text (p.153)
- Internal corporate infographics exist because employees who understand data perform better; clear information reduces mistakes, misunderstandings, and misjudgements (p.179)
- The **Picture Superiority Effect**: audiences remember visual information better than text. Charts must be specific to the data to trigger this effect — generic PowerPoint templates undermine it (p.197–198)
- Visuals communicate data to employees faster and more memorably than standard reports. A research report with 100 charts using identical styling becomes impossible to remember (p.179)

## Common Mistakes and Anti-Patterns

- **Pie charts that do not add up to 100%**: the most common infographic error. Pie charts show parts of a whole; using them for independent frequency data (e.g. multi-select survey questions) is wrong. Values must sum to 100% (p.199–200)
- **Maslow's Hammer**: designers reach for the familiar chart type (pie/bar) even when it is the wrong tool for the data (p.200)
- **Qualitative data shown as quantitative**: using a pie chart showing "66%" when only 6 people were interviewed creates a false impression of statistical validity. Better: show 4 out of 6 people icons (p.193–194)
- **Dark-background designs for print**: infographic resumes and presentations with dark/black backgrounds are hard to print and many recruiters will avoid printing them (p.162)
- **All presentations using identical templates**: when a company mandates a single PowerPoint template, every chart looks the same — none are memorable (p.179)
- **Clustered bar charts for multi-dimensional comparison**: 21 data points across 3 brands and 7 attributes crammed into a clustered bar chart is hard to parse (p.189–190)

## Marks and Channels

- **Proportionally sized circles**: encode magnitude through area; easy to compare relative size; work well for hierarchical budget data where circles are nested (parent category = large circle, subcategories = satellite circles) (p.181–182)
- **Color on circle outlines/arrows**: encodes direction of change — red = decreased budget, yellow = unchanged, green = increased. This adds a third variable (change direction) without adding clutter (p.181–182)
- **Proportionally sized rectangles (treemap)**: area encodes magnitude; enables hierarchical nesting where sub-items fill parent rectangles (p.182–183)
- **Opposing horizontal bars**: two bar charts back-to-back from a central item axis, encoding two related but distinct quantities (sales vs. profit) simultaneously. Not a default Excel chart type — must be built manually (p.183–184)
- **Proportionally sized logo circles**: logo images placed inside circles sized to represent sales volume — communicates category identity through the logo itself while size encodes magnitude (p.184–185)
- **10×10 icon/square grids**: 100 squares, color-filled to percentage value. Immediately readable because humans are Base-10 intuitive. Rows of 10 only — never 12, 17, 20, 24 (p.190–192)
- **People icons (isotype-style)**: literal count of individuals represented as person-shaped icons. Each icon = one person. Color distinguishes groups. Keeps sample size visible and prevents false quantitative impression (p.193–194)
- **Word clouds**: font size encodes word frequency. Color distinguishes sentiment groups (green = positive, red = negative). No precise values shown — purely qualitative/directional (p.194–195)
- **Font size as importance channel**: in process timelines, label font size represents relative importance of a milestone (p.186)
- **Circle size as involvement channel**: in project timelines, circle diameter encodes estimated involvement level (p.186)

## Task-Encoding Fit

| User task | Encoding that works | Notes from book |
|---|---|---|
| Compare magnitudes (budget, sales) | Proportional circles or bars | Circles show hierarchy well (p.181) |
| Show part-of-whole | Treemap or stacked bar | NOT pie charts for multi-select data (p.200) |
| Show two correlated quantities | Back-to-back opposing bars | Sales vs. profit per product (p.183) |
| Show categorical magnitude with identity | Sized logo circles | No axis needed; logos carry meaning (p.185) |
| Compare percentages across categories and brands | 10×10 grids (small multiples) | 21 data points readable on one page (p.191) |
| Show small sample qualitative data | Person icons (literal count) | Never use pie chart for n<30 (p.193) |
| Show sentiment from text | Dual word clouds | Positive/negative colour split (p.194) |
| Show process/sequence | Illustrated path or enriched flowchart | Character-based path aids employee identification (p.187) |

## Data Manipulation Worth Visualizing

- **Budget split: revenue vs. expenditure** — visualising as two halves of a symmetric radial diagram makes surplus/deficit immediately clear (p.180)
- **Year-over-year change** — encoding percentage change as arrow color (red/yellow/green) adds temporal comparison without a second chart (p.181)
- **Profit margin vs. revenue** — these are often confused internally; a dual-axis opposing bar chart separates them and reveals that highest-revenue products are not always most profitable (p.183)
- **Statistical significance in survey data** — small colored dots beneath each 10×10 grid indicate which brands scored significantly differently; this is a very compact encoding of inferential statistics (p.191)

## Design Process Guidance

- **Accuracy first, aesthetics second**: one wrong chart destroys the credibility of everything else in the infographic. Audiences assume if one thing is wrong, everything is wrong (p.199)
- **Make every chart relevant to the specific data**: change colors to match the brand/subject; label values inside bars; remove gridlines and axis lines for simple charts; use logos instead of legends (p.197–198)
- **Design for print from the start**: test on lowest-quality printer, in black and white, at minimum resolution. Many recruiters and HR managers print on office printers (p.162)
- **One-page constraint**: for handouts and resumes, the single page forces prioritisation. Most important information must be visible without scrolling or flipping (p.196)
- **Combined infographic + text resume**: embed data visualisations directly into the text resume rather than as a separate document; this minimises eye movement and keeps visual context adjacent to detail (p.157–158)
- **Publishing infographic resumes online**: host on a personal landing page; share via LinkedIn, social media; always make a PDF available for download; ensure the Microsoft Word version also exists for job application systems (p.160–161)

---

## Interaction Design

- **Prezi zooming interface (ZUI)**: enables a single large infographic canvas to serve as both a printed handout and a presentation. The presenter defines a path through the canvas; the software animates zoom/pan between points. Audience follows along on their printed copy (p.196–197)
- **Linked charts in Excel**: if data visualisations embedded in a resume are Excel charts linked to a data source, updates propagate automatically (p.158)
- **PDF interactive links**: in iPad/tablet-format infographic resumes, logos and icons can be tappable links (e-mail, LinkedIn profile). No additional app required (p.163–164)

## Practical Rules of Thumb

- Pie chart slices must always sum to 100%. If your data comes from a multi-select question, use a bar chart instead (p.199–200)
- For icon/shape rows, always use rows of 10 — never 12, 17, 20, or 24 (p.191)
- Do not use qualitative sample data (n<30) with quantitative chart types; visualise the literal count with person icons instead (p.193)
- Word clouds are qualitative and show general sentiment themes — they do not show precise frequencies and should not be treated as statistically significant (p.195)
- Infographic resumes should always have a text-only companion for job application systems that parse text; the infographic attracts attention but the text provides the searchable content (p.153, 157)
- Design for A4/letter size proportions, not tall-format web infographics — tall formats do not print well on standard office printers (p.161)
- Test color-dependent designs in grayscale: blue elements convert well; bright colour-only encoding fails (p.161–162)
