# [agent_03] Cool Infographics — pages 101-150

## Infographic CV / Visual Resume Ideas

Pages 131–150 constitute an entire chapter (Chapter 4) dedicated to infographic resumes. This is the single most directly relevant section of the book for the CV goal. Multiple real, detailed examples are shown and analyzed. All ideas below are extracted from this chapter.

---

### CV Idea: Layered Area Chart Career Timeline (p.132)
- **What it visualizes:** Work history + education history on a single horizontal time axis; concurrent activities shown as overlapping colored area bands
- **How it works:** X-axis = years (e.g., 2010–2025 for our context). Above the date line: work experience shown as overlapping colored areas, each color = a role domain (sales, software development, data science studies). Below the date line: education phases (Psychology degree, current AI/data science studies). The Y-axis is labeled as a qualitative variable like "relative engagement level." No numeric values on Y-axis — this is intentional.
- **Adaptation for our context:** This is perfect for the psychology → sales → software development → data science trajectory. The overlapping areas would show that software development began while still in a sales role (concurrent learning), and data science studies overlap with current work. The "below the line" education section shows the two degrees. Color coding: psychology phase (e.g., purple), sales phase (orange), software development (blue), data science trajectory (teal). The area widths growing rightward show increasing technical depth over time. A small donut or pie chart beside the main timeline shows the current skill portfolio (angle = personal time invested per domain; height/radius = professional readiness).
- **Page reference:** p.132 (Michael Anderson infographic resume)

### CV Idea: Dual-Column Butterfly Spine Timeline (p.134, p.141)
- **What it visualizes:** Education on one side, professional experience on the other, mirrored around a central year spine
- **How it works:** Central vertical spine with year markers (e.g., 2015 to 2025). Left side: education events shown as horizontal colored bars (Psychology degree, software self-study, current data science course). Right side: professional roles shown as horizontal bars (sales roles, commercial jobs, software development work). Bar length = duration. Bar color = domain category. Personal milestones (e.g., "Started coding," "Switched to tech") appear as small callout labels on the far left margin — as Duncan McKean's design does, including personal narrative context.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The butterfly structure is ideal for this career story because it makes the "two worlds" (academic/learning on the left, professional/commercial on the right) immediately visible. The pivot point where both sides meet a new domain (data science) would appear as a bright new color appearing simultaneously on both sides (studying AI + applying it), making the career transition visually legible as a convergence event. The inclusion of personal notes ("Discovered I liked building things," "First ML project") adds the human/personality layer that sets infographic CVs apart.
- **Page reference:** p.134 (Faz Besharatian), p.141 (Duncan McKean)

### CV Idea: Full-Page Stacked Area Timeline with Skill Domain Accumulation (p.140)
- **What it visualizes:** Entire career as a stacked area chart where total height = accumulated experience; each color band = a skill domain growing or shrinking over time
- **How it works:** X-axis = years (2015–2025+). Y-axis = total accumulated experience (grows over time, never shrinks). Color bands: Social/Sales skills (large at first, stable), Analytical/Research skills (growing steadily since psychology), Software/Technical skills (growing steeply from ~2020), AI/Data Science (newest, currently rising band at the top). Background vertical gray columns mark key life phases or locations (e.g., "University," "Sales role," "Tech career").
- **Adaptation for our context:** This design makes the "growth toward data science" narrative extremely powerful. The rising data science band at the top is visually the newest, smallest, but steepest-growing band — communicating ambition and trajectory, not just current state. The social/sales band being wide and stable (never disappearing) shows that soft skills are foundational, not replaced. The psychological research skills (analytical thinking) sit as a persistent middle band, connecting the psychology past to the data science future. Key for ML6: the stacking shows complementarity of skills, not a complete pivot.
- **Page reference:** p.140 (Mike Wirth resume)

### CV Idea: Relative Skill Bar Chart — No Numbers, Just Relative Comparison (p.141)
- **What it visualizes:** Relative proficiency across a curated list of skills, shown as unlabeled bars of varying height (or width), grouped by domain
- **How it works:** Vertical bars of varying height (Hana Tesar style, p.141). Groups: Social Skills (negotiation, client management, communication), Analytical Skills (research methods, data analysis, Python/R basics), Technical Skills (software development, web, databases), Creativity (design thinking, presentation, storytelling). Within each group, individual skills are shown as bars. Tallest bars = strongest skills. No Y-axis values shown — the comparison is relative within and across groups.
- **Adaptation for our context:** For the ML6 internship application, the highest bars in "Social Skills" (negotiation, commercial intelligence) and "Analytical Skills" (research, data thinking) signal that this candidate brings unusual soft skill depth to a technical role. A secondary text column beside the bars (like Navdeep Raj's "More" column, p.141) can list additional tools/frameworks without cluttering the visual. The honest absence of numbers prevents false precision on self-assessed skill levels — a professional signal in itself.
- **Page reference:** p.141 (Hana Tesar vertical bar resume, Navdeep Raj "More" column)

### CV Idea: Metaphor-Driven Personality Glyph — Brain + Growth Metaphor (p.142)
- **What it visualizes:** Personality traits, values, and soft skills as annotated callout labels from a central metaphor illustration
- **How it works:** A central illustration (e.g., a growing plant — appropriate given the coffee/ecology study context — or a human figure) with labeled callout lines pointing outward. Left side callouts: soft skills and personality traits (social intelligence, adaptability, creativity, negotiation). Right side callouts: technical/analytical traits (structured thinking, pattern recognition, curiosity, systems thinking). Language proficiency shown as small horizontal bars below the illustration.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Ana Frazao's design (p.142) uses a heart (passions) and brain (analytical traits) as two separate illustration anchors. For our candidate, a single growing-plant metaphor could unify the narrative: "roots" = foundational psychology and social skills; "trunk" = commercial/sales experience; "branches" = software and data skills currently growing; "new shoots" = AI/ML direction. This metaphor is particularly resonant given the data project involves actual plants. The design would be unusual, memorable, and show creative thinking — all assets for a data science application.
- **Page reference:** p.142 (Ana Foureaux Frazao heart/brain metaphor resume)

### CV Idea: Achievement Metrics as Large Typographic Numbers (p.146)
- **What it visualizes:** Quantified accomplishments from commercial/sales roles, shown as large, prominent numbers with brief explanatory captions
- **How it works:** 3–5 key metrics shown as large bold numbers (e.g., "€X revenue managed," "12 clients," "30% growth"). Each number has a 1-line caption beneath. Arranged in a row or grid. Background color blocks or icon markers differentiate the metrics. Beside the metrics: 2–3 short pull-quote style testimonials or endorsements from LinkedIn recommendations (attributed to named people with their roles).
- **Adaptation for our context:** For a candidate with sales/commercial experience, this section converts soft-skill-heavy experience into hard data. Quantified sales results are the most convincing evidence of commercial effectiveness for an employer. Pairing the numbers with a brief personality sentence ("I approach data the same way I approached sales — by understanding what the other person actually needs") creates a bridge narrative from sales to data science. This section would be placed immediately below the name/header, before the timeline, to create a strong first impression within the six-second window.
- **Page reference:** p.146 (David P. Ingram achievement metrics section)

### CV Idea: Software Application Icons as Skill Credentials (p.146)
- **What it visualizes:** Technical tool proficiency shown through recognizable software/platform icons instead of or alongside text labels
- **How it works:** A row or grid of software icons (Python snake logo, R logo, Jupyter, Pandas, scikit-learn, SQL, Git, Tableau, etc.) grouped by domain. Below each icon: a relative proficiency bar (short = basic, long = advanced). The icon does the heavy lifting of identification; the bar adds the proficiency dimension. Icons for soft-skill tools (LinkedIn, Slack) or educational platforms (Coursera, Kaggle) can appear in a separate "Learning" section.
- **Adaptation for our context:** For an ML6 internship application, showing Kaggle, Python, scikit-learn, and Pandas icons signals data science fluency visually. The relative proficiency bars acknowledge that some tools are more advanced than others — which is more credible than claiming equal mastery of all. Including Kaggle as an icon with a "rank/percentile" annotation adds a verifiable credential that is uncommon in standard resumes.
- **Page reference:** p.146 (Adrian Saker logo resume, David Ingram company logos — same principle applied to software tools)

### CV Idea: Honeycomb / Hexagon Personality Map (p.146)
- **What it visualizes:** Personality traits, work style preferences, and values arranged spatially — positive traits radiating outward from center, things the candidate actively avoids pointing inward
- **How it works:** Central hexagon = candidate name/title. Surrounding hexagons (6 directions): filled with personality traits or work style attributes. Color of each hexagon = trait category (social/interpersonal, analytical, creative, technical). Size of hexagon = relative emphasis. Optional second ring: things the candidate actively dislikes or avoids in work environments (as Ingram's "I Dislike" section does) — this communicates self-awareness and honesty.
- **Adaptation for our context:** For a data science internship: inner ring = core strengths (social intelligence, analytical curiosity, creative problem-solving, structured communication). Outer ring = growing capabilities (machine learning, statistical modeling, data storytelling). Optional "I thrive when" / "I struggle with" hexagons at the edges signal emotional intelligence and self-awareness — both valued in team environments like ML6.
- **Page reference:** p.146 (David P. Ingram honeycomb "I Like / I Dislike" section)

### Key Design Principles from Chapter 4 (applicable to all CV ideas)

- **Three or fewer** data visualizations in the entire resume design — more creates visual noise and the hiring manager loses the key message (p.137)
- The infographic resume complements, not replaces, a traditional text resume — keep the text resume available for download (p.132)
- **No numeric labels** required on relative skill charts — honest acknowledgment that skill levels are subjective; absence of numbers prevents false precision (p.138, 141)
- Visualize concurrent activities with overlapping areas or dual-column layouts — sequential-only representations misrepresent how careers actually work (p.138)
- The **Picture Superiority Effect**: visualized information is remembered days later; text is forgotten within hours (p.136)
- Hiring managers spend an average of **six seconds** on a resume — the most important data must be immediately visible above the fold (p.136)
- A poor design can disqualify a candidate just as fast as a good design qualifies one — tone, complexity, and relevance must all match the target role (p.136)
