# [agent_05] Cool Infographics — pages 201-249

## Overview

Pages 201–249 are primarily about infographic design best practices and design tools rather than CV-specific content. However, several principles and a few specific visual forms are directly applicable to infographic CV design. Extracted below.

---

### CV Idea: The Credibility Footer Strip (p.217–218)

- **What it visualizes:** Contact information, credentials, and links — the "fine print" of a personal brand.
- **How it works:** Every infographic in the book includes a consistent footer containing: logo/name, landing page URL, source citations, license type, and designer credit. For a CV, this translates to a persistent bottom strip (or sidebar) with: name + photo as a "logo", LinkedIn/portfolio URL, date of last update, and a one-line positioning statement. This is always present regardless of which section of the CV is being viewed, ensuring the key contact information travels with the document when shared.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The psychology → data science pivot can be stated as a one-line "key message" in the footer strip, just as infographics state their key message within 5 seconds. Something like: "Psychology & sales background now applied to data science — currently studying ML." This ensures even readers who only skim the CV for 5 seconds get the core positioning.
- **Page reference:** p.217–218

### CV Idea: The 5-Second Key Message Banner (p.208)

- **What it visualizes:** Career positioning / professional identity — the single most important thing the reader should understand.
- **How it works:** The 5-second rule says a design must communicate its key message within 5 seconds to skimming readers. On a CV, this means placing a large, visually dominant "key message" at the top — not a generic objective statement in small font, but a visually prominent declaration that can be read at a glance. This could be a bold headline + one supporting visual (e.g., a simple skill arc or icon row) that instantly communicates the transition narrative.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The key message for this CV is the pivot: someone with strong human skills (psychology, sales) who has developed technical data science competence. A top-of-page visual block showing two "phases" (Phase 1: Psychology + Sales icons; Phase 2: Data Science + ML icons) with an arrow between them communicates the story before any text is read. Color coding the two phases differently (warm/cool) reinforces the transition visually.
- **Page reference:** p.208

### CV Idea: The Inline Label Bar Chart — Skills without a Legend (p.214–215)

- **What it visualizes:** Skill proficiency levels across multiple skill categories.
- **How it works:** Standard CV skill bars use a legend or rely on color alone to show proficiency. The book's recommendation to eliminate legends and embed labels directly into bars translates to: each skill bar has the skill name at the left, the proficiency label (e.g., "Intermediate", "Advanced") embedded inside the colored fill portion, and a small icon representing the skill category at the far left. No separate legend needed — all information is within the reader's field of view at once.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Three skill categories — soft skills (psychology/sales), analytical skills (statistics, data science), and technical skills (coding, ML) — each get a distinct color. Within each category, bars are sorted from highest to lowest proficiency. The embedded label shows the proficiency tier; the icon at the left shows the skill domain. The reader immediately sees the profile shape: soft skills all at high level, analytical skills building, technical skills growing — which tells the pivot story in a chart.
- **Page reference:** p.214–215

### CV Idea: Area-Correct Bubble Timeline — Experience Weighted by Duration (p.203–205)

- **What it visualizes:** Work and education history, weighted by time investment.
- **How it works:** A horizontal timeline where each role/degree is shown as a bubble positioned at its start date. The bubble's area (not diameter — correctly calculated as π × r²) is proportional to the duration of that role in months. Longer roles have genuinely larger bubbles. The bubble color encodes the domain (psychology, sales/commercial, software development, data science study). A label inside or beside each bubble names the role and organization.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The timeline would show: undergraduate psychology degree (large bubble, ~3 years), sales roles (medium bubbles), software development period (medium bubble), current data science study (growing bubble at the right edge — shown as partially filled or with a dotted outline to indicate "in progress"). The visual immediately shows the multi-domain breadth and the recency of the data science pivot. The correct area sizing is itself a demonstration of data literacy — a subtle but meaningful signal to a data science recruiter.
- **Page reference:** p.203–205

### CV Idea: The Character Path Diagram — Career Journey as a Narrative Flow (p.213)

- **What it visualizes:** Career trajectory as a non-linear narrative with multiple threads converging.
- **How it works:** Inspired by the Star Wars character path diagram where multiple colored lines trace each character's journey through scenes. In a career context: three colored path lines represent three threads — (1) the academic/education thread, (2) the commercial/sales thread, (3) the technical/development thread. Each path moves left-to-right through time. At key "scene hubs" (specific roles, degrees, or projects), the paths converge or share a node, showing how skills from different domains combined. The paths gradually merge into a single data science path at the right (the current moment), demonstrating that all prior experience feeds into the current trajectory.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Thread 1 (psychology/analytical): psychology degree → research methods training → data science courses. Thread 2 (commercial/social): sales roles → client negotiation → communication skills. Thread 3 (technical): software development experience → programming skills → ML coursework. The diagram shows these as separate paths in early career that progressively interweave, culminating in a "Data Scientist" node at the right edge. The narrative reads: "These experiences are not unrelated — they converged intentionally." This is semantically novel because career path diagrams almost always show linear progression; this shows multi-threaded convergence.
- **Page reference:** p.213
