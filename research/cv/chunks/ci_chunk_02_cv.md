### CV Idea: Multi-Column Generation Table for Career Timeline (p.95–96)

- **What it visualizes:** Work experience across multiple jobs/roles — one row per role, columns per attribute
- **How it works:** Inspired by the Honda Accord 30 Years infographic. Each row = one job/role. Columns: time period (left axis), role title (bold text), domain/industry (icon glyph), key skills gained (icon glyph set), output/achievement (short bar or number), and a "color swatch strip" showing the technology stack used (one color per tech, in a small horizontal mosaic). The vertical axis is time (oldest at top). Connecting lines between rows in the same domain link related experiences.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The psychology → sales → software → data science trajectory maps perfectly onto this format. Each generation (role) shows what skills were added. The "color swatch" column becomes a tech/skill mosaic that gets progressively more technical as the timeline descends toward the present. The connecting lines show which skills transferred from one role to the next (e.g., psychology → social intelligence → sales → negotiation → software). This makes the career pivot look like an evolution rather than a discontinuity.
- **Page reference:** p.95–96

### CV Idea: Side-by-Side "Then vs. Now" Profile Comparison (p.93–94)

- **What it visualizes:** Career pivot — showing the two "versions" of the person across 5–6 dimensions
- **How it works:** Inspired by the SoNice "Making an Organic Choice" two-column comparison. Left column = "Background" (psychology, sales, social intelligence, commercial work). Right column = "Now" (data science, analytical thinking, ML, programming). Center column = labeled dimension buttons: "Problem-solving approach", "Core tools", "Communication style", "Sector", "Learning mode", "Output type". Each dimension uses a mini-bar or dot to show presence/strength on each side.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The left column is not a weakness — it is shown as the foundation. The format makes it visually clear that the right column BUILDS on the left rather than replacing it. For example: "Communication style" — Left: negotiation and persuasion / Right: data storytelling and visualization. This reframes the career pivot as a deliberate expansion rather than a random change. The colors should be chosen so neither side looks dominant — the message is synthesis, not replacement.
- **Page reference:** p.93–94

### CV Idea: Monroe's Motivated Sequence CV Narrative (p.85–86)

- **What it visualizes:** Career story structured as a persuasive argument
- **How it works:** Inspired by the "Can Soap Make You Sick?" persuasive infographic. The CV is structured as a top-to-bottom narrative: (1) KEY MESSAGE — headline about what you offer ("A people-scientist becoming a data scientist"). (2) PROBLEM — "AI/data science needs people who understand human behavior, not just algorithms." (3) WHY YOU — your psychology background as the differentiator. (4) PROOF — timeline of roles with key metrics (deals closed, projects shipped). (5) CALL TO ACTION — "Let's talk" with contact details and a link to portfolio.
- **Adaptation for our context:** This format works especially well for a non-traditional applicant who needs to reframe their background as an asset. The persuasive structure prevents the reader from dismissing the psychology/sales background as irrelevant — instead it builds the case that these skills are exactly what distinguishes you from a conventional CS graduate. The data visualizations (timeline bars, skill levels) serve as the "quantitative credibility" that the book mentions data visualizations always imply.
- **Page reference:** p.85–86

### CV Idea: Petal/Wheel Chart of Skill Dimensions (p.67)

- **What it visualizes:** Skills profile across multiple dimensions simultaneously
- **How it works:** Inspired by the "Mobile Youth" communication wheel. A radial chart with 6–8 petals, each representing a skill dimension (e.g., Statistical Analysis, Machine Learning, Data Visualization, Communication, Negotiation, Programming, Domain Knowledge, Creativity). Petal length = self-assessed proficiency level (1–5 scale). Each petal is color-coded by skill category (technical = blue, interpersonal = orange, creative = green). The chart shows the full profile at a glance — a conventional radar chart, but with petal shape rather than polygon fill for a more distinctive look.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The multi-dimensional nature of this chart is ideal for showing that the candidate is not a narrow technical specialist. The interpersonal and creative petals being full-length, alongside partially-developed technical petals (currently studying), accurately represents the profile. A caption can note "in progress" for technical skills. This is more honest and more visually interesting than a simple skill bar list.
- **Page reference:** p.67

### CV Idea: Radial Taxonomy of Skills and Experiences (p.100)

- **What it visualizes:** Full ecosystem of skills, experiences, and tools organized by category
- **How it works:** Inspired by "The Conversation Prism" radial category map. A circular layout where the center = "YOU" (name + headline). Radiating sectors = skill/experience categories (Psychology & Behavior, Sales & Commercial, Software Development, Data Science & ML, Communication, Languages). Within each sector, individual items (specific skills, tools, certifications, experiences) are placed as labeled dots or small text badges at varying distances from center. Items closer to center = more developed/central. Color = category sector.
- **Adaptation for our context:** This format makes an unusually diverse background look like a rich ecosystem rather than a scattered history. The center-out distance (closeness to YOU) can encode how central each skill is to your current identity — so data science tools sit closest, while early sales experience is at the periphery but still present. The full 360 view communicates breadth while the distance axis communicates depth and current focus. Works best as a one-page A4 visual centerpiece.
- **Page reference:** p.100

### CV Idea: Tall Vertical Timeline as Career Growth Narrative (p.67–72)

- **What it visualizes:** Career trajectory over time — education, roles, skills gained per period
- **How it works:** Inspired by the tall/vertical online infographic format discussion. A single tall vertical timeline (top = start, bottom = now/applying). Each period is a horizontal band containing: (left) role/institution name, (center) key visual — a small bar chart of skills gained during that period, (right) an icon glyph representing the domain (brain = psychology, handshake = sales, code brackets = software, data chart = data science). Bands grow progressively wider as more skills accumulate — the widening shape of the timeline itself encodes career growth.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The widening shape is semantically meaningful for this specific career: each role added something new rather than replacing the previous. The small bar charts within each band show which skills spiked during that period. A connecting line of color-coded "transferable skills" runs down the center, showing which capabilities (communication, analytical thinking, problem-solving) thread through every role. Ends with the current studying period and a "trajectory arrow" pointing to the target internship domain.
- **Page reference:** p.67–72

### CV Idea: Informative "About Me" Mini-Infographic (p.72–78)

- **What it visualizes:** Personal data and character traits presented as a data story
- **How it works:** Inspired by the informative infographic category — valuable information without overt sales pitch. A one-page design presenting personal/professional data: number of countries visited (map dot), languages spoken (bar), books read in past year (icon array), domains of expertise (Venn diagram of 3 overlapping circles: people / data / systems), years of experience per domain (numbers), and one striking personal statistic ("Closed X deals worth €Y in sales career" or "Built X software projects"). Ends with contact/portfolio link but no explicit call to purchase.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The Venn diagram of People × Data × Systems is the key visual for this persona — it shows that the three domains overlap in this candidate's profile, which is the core message. The personal statistics (sales deals, projects shipped) serve as the data-credibility layer. By framing personal facts as data, the design signals that the candidate thinks in data — appropriate for a data science internship application.
- **Page reference:** p.72–78
