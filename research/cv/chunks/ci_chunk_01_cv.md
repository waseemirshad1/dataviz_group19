### CV Idea: The Three-Part Story Resume (inspired by p.35–37)

- **What it visualizes:** The overall narrative arc of the career — not just a list of jobs, but a story with introduction, main event, and call to action
- **How it works:** Structure the CV as three visual zones:
  1. *Introduction/Foundation* (top section): Who am I and why should you care? Short visual summary — degree(s), current study, one-line career pivot statement. A small timeline or two key credentials shown as visual anchors.
  2. *Ah-Ha! The Main Event* (center, dominant): The unusual combination — psychology background + software skills + data science pivot. This is where the most visually distinctive element lives. Could be a skill radar, a "dual-track" visual showing the analytical + interpersonal axes, or a data science project highlight visualized (e.g., a mini chart of a real result).
  3. *Conclusion/Call-to-Action* (bottom): "Here's what I want to do and how to reach me." Contact info, LinkedIn, GitHub, portfolio link — made visually prominent.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The psychology→sales→coding→data science arc is the story. The introduction establishes the breadth; the main event makes the unusual combination the selling point rather than an apology; the call-to-action is the internship ask.
- **Page reference:** p.35–37

### CV Idea: Contextual Comparison for Skills — "I'm Not Just a Psych Grad" (inspired by p.29–31)

- **What it visualizes:** Skill levels, but placed in context that makes them meaningful rather than as isolated bars
- **How it works:** Instead of a plain skills bar chart (one bar = one skill, no context), show each skill in comparison to a reference — either "typical psychology graduate" or "typical data science applicant." Use proportional circles or diverging bars:
  - Left side: "typical profile" (grey)
  - Right side: "my profile" (colored)
  - The divergence makes unusual strengths immediately visible — e.g., communication/negotiation skills far exceed typical data science applicant; programming skills already above typical psychology graduate
- **Adaptation for our context:** The psychology + sales background gives genuinely unusual soft skill strengths for a data science role. Visualizing these as "above baseline" rather than as bare numbers makes the differentiation visible and argues the case visually.
- **Page reference:** p.29–31

### CV Idea: Waffle Chart Skills Grid (inspired by Picture Superiority Effect waffle chart, p.32)

- **What it visualizes:** Proficiency levels for multiple skills simultaneously, as a percentage of mastery
- **How it works:** For each skill (Python, R, SQL, Statistics, Machine Learning, Sales/Negotiation, Communication, Psychology/Research Methods), create a small 10x10 waffle grid where filled squares = proficiency percentage. Arrange 8–10 grids in a 2-column layout with skill name and % label below each. Color by skill domain (technical = blue, analytical = green, interpersonal = orange).
- **Adaptation for our context:** The waffle format is honest and readable — it doesn't exaggerate like full progress bars. The color grouping makes the three skill clusters (technical/analytical/interpersonal) visible at a glance. For a psychology→data science pivot, showing the interpersonal cluster as equally filled grids alongside the technical cluster makes the "full-stack person" argument visually.
- **Page reference:** p.32

### CV Idea: Career Timeline as Circular Timeline with Hover-Detail Layers (inspired by "A Raw Chocolate History," p.47–49)

- **What it visualizes:** Career progression over time — degrees, jobs, skill acquisitions — as a circular or spiral timeline
- **How it works:** A circular timeline (like "A Raw Chocolate History") where each segment of the arc represents a life/career phase: Psychology degree → Sales role 1 → Sales role 2 → Software development pivot → Current data science study. Icons or small illustrations at each node represent the domain. A bar chart of "data science skill growth" runs as a radial axis inward from the outer circle — showing how skills accumulated throughout, not just at formal milestones. In a static version, all key facts are visible; in an interactive version, hovering over any node reveals role title, key skills gained, and relevant soft skill used.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The circular format visually argues that the career is not a random series of jobs but a coherent arc — each phase built skills used in the next. The "chocolate purity" bar chart embedded in the original (cocoa solids % over time) translates directly to a "data science readiness" score or skill accumulation metric growing over time.
- **Page reference:** p.47–49

### CV Idea: Subway Map Career — Multiple Parallel Skill Lines Through Time (inspired by "Underskin" and "My Visual Mapping Blogroll" subway maps, p.33–34, p.50)

- **What it visualizes:** Multiple parallel skill tracks (analytical thinking, interpersonal skills, technical coding, domain knowledge) as colored subway lines running through a horizontal timeline
- **How it works:** Four colored lines run left to right across the page (timeline = X axis):
  - Orange: Interpersonal/Sales skills
  - Blue: Analytical/Research skills
  - Green: Technical/Coding skills
  - Purple: Domain knowledge (psychology → business → data science)
  
  Each job, course, or experience is a station node on the line(s) it developed. Stations where multiple lines overlap are marked as major interchange nodes — these represent the most valuable experiences (e.g., "led data-informed sales analysis" sits on the orange + blue + green lines simultaneously). The visual argument: all four lines are live throughout the career, and by the current moment, all converge at the "ML6 Internship" destination station.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The semantic novelty is strong — a subway map for a career is recognizable as a visual form but unusual for a CV. The overlapping stations make the "transferable skills" argument without text. The final convergence point (internship destination) is the call-to-action made visual.
- **Page reference:** p.33–34, p.50

### CV Idea: Unit Translation — "Skills in Human Scale" (inspired by Tower of Beer and newspaper grid, p.25, p.37–38)

- **What it visualizes:** Abstract skill claims translated into concrete, countable achievements
- **How it works:** Instead of writing "strong communication skills," convert to a unit visualization: "Presented to X clients / Negotiated Y deals / Led Z meetings" shown as icon grids (one icon = one instance). For data science: "Built X models / Processed Y datasets / Wrote Z lines of code" as similar unit grids. The grids sit next to skill labels and make the claims visceral and countable rather than vague.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The psychology + sales background generates real numbers — years of experience, number of clients, deals closed. The data science trajectory generates project counts and model metrics. Translating these into icon grids makes the depth of experience visible in a way that text cannot.
- **Page reference:** p.25, p.37–38

### CV Idea: "Picture Superiority" Profile Photo Integration (inspired by p.32–33)

- **What it visualizes:** Personal identity and memorability — the CV itself as a visual artifact
- **How it works:** Use the Picture Superiority Effect deliberately — include a professional photo not as decoration but as the primary memory anchor. Surround the photo with a visual "halo" of key traits (3–5 words or small icons representing character: Curious / Analytical / Communicator / Builder / Adaptable). Research shows that text + image is remembered at 65% vs. 10% text alone after 3 days — the recruiter who sees 50 CVs will remember the one with the strong visual identity.
- **Adaptation for our context:** For a data science role, pair the photo with a small data visualization (e.g., a mini chart from a real project) — this immediately communicates "this person thinks in data" before reading a word.
- **Page reference:** p.32–33
