# [agent_17] Visualization Analysis and Design — pages 51-100

## Note on This Chunk

Pages 51–100 focus on data abstraction, task abstraction, and the four-level validation framework. These are primarily theoretical and analytical tools. The CV ideas below translate these theoretical frameworks into self-presentation and visual communication strategies for a candidate transitioning from psychology + sales + software development into data science (targeting ML6 internship, Belgium).

---

### CV Idea: Skill-Task Matrix — Mapping Your Background to Data Science Actions (p.67–82)
- **What it visualizes:** Your competencies across domains (psychology, sales, software dev) mapped onto Munzner's task abstraction hierarchy: Discover / Present / Enjoy / Annotate / Record / Derive — Lookup / Locate / Browse / Explore — Identify / Compare / Summarize.
- **How it works:** A two-axis grid or heatmap. Rows = your skill areas (statistical reasoning from psychology, persuasion/communication from sales, coding from software dev). Columns = abstract task types. Each cell is filled with a concrete example where you performed that task type in that domain. This makes immediately visible which task types you can support across multiple domains — and which are unique to your data science training.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Instead of a generic skills list, you present yourself as someone who already operates at multiple levels of task abstraction (discovery, presentation, record/derive) across multiple domains. Recruiters see not just "I did sales" but "I practiced comparison and summarization tasks in sales" — language that resonates with data analytics job descriptions.
- **Page reference:** p.67–82 (task abstraction: analyze/search/query hierarchy; actions and targets framework).

### CV Idea: Derived-Attribute Timeline — Showing What You've Built, Not Just Where You've Been (p.75–78)
- **What it visualizes:** A timeline of your roles and projects, but instead of listing raw job titles (the original attributes), each entry shows a *derived* attribute: what problem you solved, what you discovered, what you produced. The raw job (e.g. "sales representative") is subordinate; the derived outcome (e.g. "designed customer segmentation approach that reduced churn by 12%") is primary.
- **How it works:** Horizontal timeline with each role as a node. Below each node: a small pair of bars or annotations — "Original data: [role title, tasks]" → "Derived attribute: [analytical/technical output]." Could be implemented as a compact two-row layout per role, echoing Figure 3.5's trade balance derivation (p.77).
- **Adaptation for our context:** Directly applies Munzner's insight that encoding the derived variable is preferable to asking the reader to infer it from raw data. Your reader (recruiter) should not have to compute "what transferable skill does 'sales representative' imply for ML6?" — you show the derived value explicitly. Especially powerful for non-linear career paths where the derived skills (analytical thinking, communication under uncertainty, behavioral research methods from psychology) are more relevant than the raw job titles.
- **Page reference:** p.75–78 (derive goal: produce new data from existing data; derived trade balance attribute preferable to raw imports + exports).

### CV Idea: Nested Level Profile — Showing You Understand the Full Stack (p.92–100)
- **What it visualizes:** A concentric/nested diagram (echoing the four nested levels of vis design: domain situation → abstraction → idiom → algorithm) adapted to your competency stack: domain knowledge (psychology, behavior, sales) → analytical abstraction (problem framing, task analysis, research design) → communication idiom (data storytelling, dashboard design, visualization) → technical implementation (Python/R, ML models, software development).
- **How it works:** Four nested rings or layers, each labeled with your competencies at that level. Arrows or annotations illustrate specific projects where you worked across all four levels (i.e. you understood a domain problem, abstracted it, chose the right encoding, and implemented it efficiently).
- **Adaptation for our context:** The nested model metaphor signals to a technically sophisticated recruiter (ML6 internship) that you understand the difference between domain understanding and algorithmic implementation — a rare combination. It also visually demonstrates that you are not just a coder or just a domain expert but someone who bridges all four levels, which is what data science internships actually need.
- **Page reference:** p.92–100 (four nested levels: domain → abstraction → idiom → algorithm; angles of attack: problem-driven vs. technique-driven).

### CV Idea: What-Why-How Grid for Key Projects (p.84–86, p.68–71)
- **What it visualizes:** For 2–3 key projects (one per domain: psychology research, sales analytics, software project), a compact three-column annotation block: "What (data)" → "Why (task / user need)" → "How (method / tool / output)." Directly mirrors Munzner's what–why–how analysis framework.
- **How it works:** Each project is summarized in a three-cell row. "What" describes the data type and scale. "Why" describes the abstract task (discover, compare, summarize, present) and who needed it. "How" describes the specific technical or analytical method used. This replaces the standard bullet-point job description with a structured micro-analysis that demonstrates analytical thinking.
- **Adaptation for our context:** ML6 and other data science internships value candidates who can frame problems analytically before reaching for tools. Structuring your project descriptions as what-why-how demonstrates immediately that you think like a data scientist rather than just listing tools. It also mirrors the vocabulary used in data science job descriptions and academic papers — signaling cultural and intellectual fit.
- **Page reference:** p.68–71 (why analyze tasks abstractly; what-why-how framework as analytical tool), p.84–86 (comparative what-why-how analysis of SpaceTree vs. TreeJuxtaposer as a worked example).

### CV Idea: Threat-to-Validity Risk Table — Turning Self-Awareness into a Strength (p.99–100)
- **What it visualizes:** A small, honest two-column table: "Potential concern a recruiter might have" (analogous to threats to validity) → "How I address it" (analogous to validation approaches). Based on the four threat levels: wrong problem (mismatch with role needs), wrong abstraction (skills framing), wrong idiom (communication style), wrong algorithm (execution speed / tool proficiency).
- **How it works:** Compact table at the bottom or back of the CV, framed as a "Why hire me?" section. Row 1: Domain mismatch concern (non-CS background) → Evidence of domain bridging (psychology research methods, behavioral data analysis). Row 2: Abstraction concern (can you frame ML problems?) → Evidence of analytical thinking. Row 3: Presentation concern (can you communicate findings?) → Sales and communication background. Row 4: Execution concern (tool proficiency) → Software development background + specific tool list.
- **Adaptation for our context:** This is semantically novel — taking a validation framework from a visualization textbook and using it as a self-presentation structure signals deep internalization of analytical thinking. It also proactively addresses the most common objections to a non-traditional data science candidate, reducing cognitive load for the recruiter. The four-level structure maps almost perfectly onto the four main challenges of a psychology+sales+software→data science transition.
- **Page reference:** p.99–100 (threats to validity at each of the four nested levels; Figure 4.4 and Figure 4.5).
