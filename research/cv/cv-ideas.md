## Design Principles to Keep in Mind

Before the idea cards: a few cross-cutting principles extracted from the source material that constrain all CV design decisions.

- **Six seconds:** Hiring managers spend an average of 6 seconds on a first pass. The most critical data must be visible above the fold (CI p.136).
- **Three visualizations max:** No more than three distinct chart types in a single resume design — beyond this, visual noise defeats the purpose (CI p.137).
- **Function first, form next:** Every visual element must encode information. Decorative progress-circle arcs and icon grids that encode nothing are anti-patterns. Bars outperform circles for magnitude comparisons (VAD p.140–141, p.101).
- **Get it right in black and white:** All visual designs must survive grayscale printing. Use luminance (ordered) for proficiency and hue only for categorical grouping (VAD p.140, p.224).
- **Derive, do not list:** Encode derived attributes (what you built, what you demonstrated) rather than raw job titles (VAD p.75–78). The reader should not have to infer transferable value — show it directly.
- **The infographic CV complements, not replaces, a plain-text CV** — keep the ATS-readable version available for download (CI p.132).
- **No numeric labels required on relative skill charts** — honest acknowledgment that self-assessment is subjective; the absence of false precision is itself a professional signal (CI p.138, 141).

## Section 1 — Education / Degrees

### CV Idea: Dual-Column Butterfly Spine Timeline
- **What it visualizes:** Education (left) and professional experience (right) mirrored around a central vertical year spine
- **How it works:** Central vertical spine with year markers (e.g., 2015–2025). Left side: education events as horizontal colored bars (Psychology degree, self-directed software study, current data science course), bar length = duration, bar color = domain. Right side: professional roles as horizontal bars (sales roles, commercial jobs, software development work). Personal milestone labels on the far left margin add the human/personality layer.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The butterfly structure makes the "two worlds" (academic/learning left, professional/commercial right) immediately visible. The pivot point appears as a bright new color on both sides simultaneously — studying AI while still working — making the transition legible as a deliberate convergence event. Including personal notes ("Discovered I liked building things," "First ML project") distinguishes the infographic CV from a standard one.
- **Page reference:** CI p.134 (Faz Besharatian), CI p.141 (Duncan McKean)

### CV Idea: Multi-Ring Radial Timeline — Degrees / Roles / Skills as Concentric Layers
- **What it visualizes:** Career and education history as concentric rings on a radial time axis
- **How it works:** Time runs around the circle (arc from 2018 to present). Innermost ring = life timeline. Second ring = education arcs (colored by domain). Third ring = work experience arcs. Outermost ring = skill acquisition — each skill appears as a small arc in the year it was first used, with connection lines to the degree/job that produced it.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Psychology background occupies a large arc of the education ring. Software development fills an adjacent work arc. The pivot is visible as the outer skill rings growing outward: early = psychology methods; mid = programming languages; recent = ML tools. The data science / ML6 entry appears at the outer edge as the most densely filled recent arc, showing accelerated growth.
- **Page reference:** DS p.389–400

### CV Idea: Layered Area Chart Career Timeline
- **What it visualizes:** Full career as overlapping colored area bands on a horizontal time axis; education shown below the line, work experience above
- **How it works:** X-axis = years. Above the date line: work experience as overlapping colored areas per domain (sales, software development, data science studies). Below the date line: education phases (Psychology degree, current AI studies). Y-axis is qualitative "relative engagement level" — no numeric values needed. Overlapping areas show concurrent activities.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The overlapping areas show that software development began while still in a sales role (concurrent learning), and data science studies overlap with current work. The below-line education section shows the two degrees clearly. A donut beside the main timeline shows current skill portfolio by domain. This is the format most commonly recommended for multi-phase career timelines in the source material.
- **Page reference:** CI p.132 (Michael Anderson infographic resume)

## Section 2 — Skills (Technical and Soft)

### CV Idea: Inline Label Bar Chart — Skills Grouped by Domain, No Separate Legend
- **What it visualizes:** Skill proficiency levels across all domains, encoded as horizontal bars sorted by relevance to the target role
- **How it works:** Three categorical groups (soft/psychology/sales, analytical, technical) each with a distinct color hue. Within each group, bars are sorted from highest to lowest proficiency. The skill name is at the left; the proficiency label ("Intermediate", "Advanced") is embedded inside the colored fill portion; a small domain icon appears at the far left. No separate legend. Order bars top-to-bottom by ML6 relevance, not alphabetically.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The profile shape across all three groups tells the story: soft skills universally at high level (long bars), analytical skills building (medium bars), technical skills growing (shorter bars, still visible). The recruiter reads the transition without a single word of explanation. Use luminance within each hue group (not rainbow) to distinguish proficiency levels. Must read in grayscale.
- **Page reference:** CI p.214–215, VAD p.101–102, VAD p.140

### CV Idea: Dual Concentric Ring Skill Glyph — Mastery vs. Frequency of Use
- **What it visualizes:** For each key skill: both mastery level (how advanced) and current usage frequency (how actively applied)
- **How it works:** Each skill shown as two concentric donuts. Outer ring arc filled clockwise = expertise level (0–100%). Inner ring arc filled clockwise = frequency of use. Labels below each glyph: level category (Pro/Advanced/Intermediate/Beginner) and frequency (Always/Usually/Regular/Sometimes). A star marks top skills.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Python (outer: advanced, inner: daily), Statistics (outer: advanced, inner: always), ML fundamentals (outer: intermediate, inner: regular), Sales CRM tools (outer: expert, inner: now rarely). The divergence between expertise and current frequency is honest and interesting — it signals which skills are being actively grown versus which are legacy skills from prior career phases. Directly communicates the transition to a data-literate recruiter.
- **Page reference:** CI p.173

### CV Idea: Overlapping Skill Bubble Clusters
- **What it visualizes:** Skills and competencies with relative importance shown through bubble size; cluster proximity shows conceptual relationships
- **How it works:** Circles of varying sizes represent skill areas. Size = relative proficiency or importance. Overlap between circles = conceptual relationship. No axis needed. Cluster 1 (large): Data Science, Python, Machine Learning. Cluster 2 (large): Communication, Negotiation, Sales. Cluster 3 (medium): Psychology, Research Methods, Statistics. Cluster 4 (small, overlapping with all): Analytical Mindset, Creativity.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The overlap between the Data Science cluster and the Communication cluster is the unique selling point — few data scientists have strong commercial communication skills. The cross-cluster bridges (Analytical Mindset appearing between all three primary clusters) visually argue that the pivot is coherent, not random. The bubble format avoids false precision without sacrificing the ability to compare relative strengths.
- **Page reference:** CI p.154, CI p.162 (Jack Hagley version)

### CV Idea: Skill Radar / Profile Fingerprint — Candidate vs. Baseline Overlay
- **What it visualizes:** Multidimensional skills profile across 6–8 domains, with an overlay showing a typical data science candidate baseline for comparison
- **How it works:** Spider/radar chart with one axis per domain (psychology/behavioral research, statistics, ML, software engineering, sales/communication, data visualization, domain knowledge). The filled area shows current competence. A second lighter overlay shows a typical entry-level data science candidate baseline. The gap where the candidate exceeds the baseline = the argument.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Psychology and communication axes will visually exceed the baseline — this asymmetry is the core argument: "I bring skills ML6 candidates rarely have." Technical axes show ongoing growth. Label each axis with a specific example (e.g., "behavioral research methods", "B2B client relationships") rather than abstract category names. Note: be aware radar charts have perceptual issues (area distortion) — use as a qualitative portrait, not precise comparison.
- **Page reference:** VAD p.52, CI p.67

### CV Idea: Skills Cluster Heatmap — Domain × Competency Matrix
- **What it visualizes:** A compact matrix showing each past experience (rows) against competency dimensions required for data science (columns), with cell color saturation encoding depth of experience
- **How it works:** 4–5 rows (psychology studies, sales role, software dev projects, data viz coursework) × 6 columns (statistics, programming, data viz, user research, communication, ML/modeling). Cell color: white = no exposure, light blue = awareness, mid blue = proficiency, dark blue = strength. 3–4 saturation levels within perceptual discriminability limits.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The heatmap shows that no cell is empty — every experience contributed something to the data science profile, countering the "career change" narrative by showing additive depth. For ML6, the Statistics and Data Visualization columns should be the darkest (highest proficiency). Matrix reordering by similarity can reveal that even sales experience clusters with communication and analytical competencies required for ML consulting. Compact enough to fit in a sidebar.
- **Page reference:** VAD p.106–108, VAD p.158–161, CI p.219 (vad_chunk_05)

### CV Idea: Scented Skill Bars — Proficiency with Reference Distribution
- **What it visualizes:** Standard proficiency bars augmented with a background showing where the claimed skill level sits within a reference distribution of data science candidates
- **How it works:** Each skill bar has a thin background histogram (or light gray gradient) showing where this proficiency level sits in a reference population (e.g., from Stack Overflow Developer Survey). The saturated foreground bar = self-assessed level. A small label can say "Top 20% data science candidates" for top skills. Skill name at left, proficiency tier embedded in fill, domain icon at far left.
- **Adaptation for our context:** This transforms a static "I rate myself 4/5" claim into a contextualized, data-informed signal — exactly the kind of quantitative thinking ML6 values. Using a public reference (Stack Overflow data) makes the comparison verifiable, not just asserted. Demonstrates both visualization skill and data literacy simultaneously. Only apply to 3–5 skills where the comparison is genuinely strong.
- **Page reference:** VAD p.328

### CV Idea: Annotation-First Scatter — Skills vs. ML6 Role Demand
- **What it visualizes:** ~15–20 skills plotted in 2D space: x-axis = proficiency (self-assessed 1–5), y-axis = relevance to ML6 role (estimated from job description 1–5). Each dot = one skill. Size = years of experience. Color = domain (technical / communication / domain knowledge).
- **How it works:** Upper-right quadrant = ready-to-deploy skills (core value proposition). Lower-right = high-relevance skills being developed (honest growth narrative). Upper-left = unique differentiators from psychology/sales background (high proficiency, possibly undervalued by typical ML candidates). Heavy editorial annotations direct the recruiter's eye to the most important quadrants.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Psychology methods (experiment design, statistical inference, behavioral analysis) sit in the upper-left with annotation: "Understanding user behavior — rare in ML candidates." Python/ML frameworks sit in the lower-right with annotation linking to current coursework completion dates. A reference bubble in the upper-right corner shows the target zone derived from the ML6 job description. The annotation-first approach lets you editorially control where the recruiter looks.
- **Page reference:** DS p.363, DS p.399, VAD p.146–148

## Section 3 — Work Experience / Job Timeline

### CV Idea: Full-Page Stacked Area Timeline — Skill Domain Accumulation
- **What it visualizes:** Entire career as a stacked area chart: total height = accumulated experience (grows over time, never shrinks); each color band = a skill domain
- **How it works:** X-axis = years. Y-axis = total accumulated experience (grows monotonically). Color bands (bottom to top): Social/Sales skills (large at first, stable), Analytical/Research skills (growing steadily since psychology degree), Software/Technical skills (growing steeply from pivot point), AI/Data Science (newest, steepest-rising band at the top). Background vertical gray columns mark key life phases.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The rising data science band at the top is the newest and steepest — communicating ambition and trajectory. The social/sales band being wide and stable (never disappearing) shows soft skills are foundational, not replaced. The psychological research skills sit as a persistent middle band, connecting the psychology past to the data science future. The stacking shows complementarity of skills rather than a complete career break.
- **Page reference:** CI p.140 (Mike Wirth resume)

---

### CV Idea: Subway Map Career — Multiple Parallel Skill Lines Through Time
- **What it visualizes:** Multiple parallel skill tracks (analytical, interpersonal, technical, domain knowledge) as colored subway lines running through a horizontal career timeline
- **How it works:** Four colored lines run left to right (timeline = X axis): Orange = interpersonal/sales skills; Blue = analytical/research skills; Green = technical/coding skills; Purple = domain knowledge (psychology → business → data science). Each job, course, or experience is a station node on the line(s) it developed. Stations where multiple lines overlap = major interchange nodes = the most valuable, transferable experiences. The visual ends with all lines converging at the ML6 internship destination.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The overlapping stations make the "transferable skills" argument without text — a role where analytical and interpersonal lines both stop is immediately more interesting than a single-line role. The final convergence point (the internship destination) is the call-to-action made visual. Semantically novel: a subway map is familiar as a form but unusual as a CV structure, and the meaning of the marks (stations = experiences, interchanges = multi-domain integration) is genuinely new.
- **Page reference:** CI p.33–34, CI p.50

### CV Idea: Derived-Attribute Timeline — Showing What You Built, Not Just Where You Were
- **What it visualizes:** Career timeline where each role entry shows a *derived* attribute (what problem you solved, what analytical output you produced) as the primary label, with the raw job title subordinate
- **How it works:** Horizontal timeline with each role as a node. Below each node: a compact pair — "Role: [title]" in small text → "Derived output: [concrete outcome, e.g., 'designed customer segmentation approach that reduced churn by 12%']" in larger text. The derived outcome is the primary encoding; the job title is supporting context.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Directly applies the principle that encoding derived variables is preferable to asking the reader to infer from raw data. The psychology degree's derived output = "hypothesis-driven research methodology"; the sales role's derived output = "behavioral pattern recognition in client decision-making." These derived labels use data science vocabulary, signaling cultural fit for ML6 even when describing non-technical roles. Prevents the recruiter from dismissing psychology/sales as irrelevant.
- **Page reference:** VAD p.75–78

### CV Idea: Seasonal Block / Activity Density Per Role
- **What it visualizes:** Each job or role as a column; within each column, stacked blocks represent activity types. Block height = estimated time allocation to that activity type. Color = activity type (technical, communication, analytical, management, research).
- **How it works:** The shift in block color profile from left (sales/psychology colors dominate) to right (technical/analytical colors dominate) tells the pivot story without a word. A consistent color legend for activity types runs across all columns. Column height = years in the role (so the reader can see relative career investment as well as activity mix).
- **Adaptation for our context:** This format encodes not just "what I did" but "how I spent my time within each role" — a much richer signal for a recruiter. The sales role shows tall communication and client-management blocks; the software development role shows tall technical and analytical blocks; the current data science study shows the target activity profile the recruiter recognizes. The leftward-to-rightward color shift is the pivot narrative.
- **Page reference:** DS p.305–306

### CV Idea: Multi-Column Generation Table — Career as Product Generations
- **What it visualizes:** Work experience in a tabular infographic format: one row per role, columns per attribute (time period, role title, domain, key skills gained, primary output, tech/skill mosaic)
- **How it works:** Inspired by product generation tables. Vertical axis = time (oldest at top). Columns: time period (left), role title (bold), domain/industry icon, key skills gained as icon glyphs, output/achievement as a short bar or metric, and a "color swatch strip" showing the skill/technology mix used (one color per tech in a small horizontal mosaic). Connecting lines between rows in the same domain link related experiences.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The color swatch mosaic column becomes progressively more technical across rows as the timeline descends toward the present. Connecting lines show which skills transferred between roles (psychology → social intelligence → sales → negotiation → software). The format makes the career pivot look like sequential product evolution rather than discontinuity — each "generation" adds features to the previous.
- **Page reference:** CI p.95–96

## Section 4 — Growth Over Time / Career Trajectory / Pivot Narrative

### CV Idea: Three Converging Career Path Lines — Multi-Threaded Path to Data Science
- **What it visualizes:** Three separate career threads converging into one data science trajectory; each thread is a colored path across a time axis
- **How it works:** Three colored path lines: Thread 1 (psychology/analytical) = psychology degree → research methods → data science courses; Thread 2 (commercial/social) = sales roles → client negotiation → communication skills; Thread 3 (technical) = software development → programming skills → ML coursework. Paths move left-to-right through time. At key "hub" events, the paths converge or share a node, showing when skills from different domains integrated. All three paths merge into the ML6 data science destination at the right edge.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The diagram argues: "These experiences are not unrelated — they converged intentionally." Semantically novel because career path diagrams almost always show linear progression; this shows multi-threaded convergence. The ML6 recruiter reads it as: someone who understood early that data science requires both human insight and technical skill, and built both deliberately. This is the single strongest narrative structure for the psychology+sales+software→data science story.
- **Page reference:** CI p.213 (Character path diagram)

### CV Idea: Streamgraph of Time × Skill Focus
- **What it visualizes:** How the proportion of active learning/experience in each domain has shifted over time, as flowing colored streams
- **How it works:** X-axis = years (~6 years). Each stream's height = fraction of active engagement in that domain at that time. Streams (bottom to top by onset time): Psychology (wide at start, narrows), Sales (mid-period peak, then narrows), Software Dev (grows and stabilizes), Data Science (grows rapidly at the right end). The flowing visual narrative shows deliberate evolution.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The most visually striking career trajectory encoding. The narrowing of psychology and sales streams does not mean those skills disappeared — it means they became a background layer, still present but no longer the primary investment. Best used as a hero/header graphic rather than within a dense CV layout. Semantically novel: streamgraphs are typically used for media consumption or financial data — using them for personal career narrative is a genuinely creative application. Note: requires the viewer to be visually literate; works well for ML6 but may confuse traditional HR.
- **Page reference:** VAD p.153–155

### CV Idea: Arc Timeline — Career Direction Score by Year
- **What it visualizes:** A year-by-year encoding of how oriented the candidate's activity was toward data science in each period
- **How it works:** X-axis = chronological years. Each year-circle is positioned by cumulative "data science orientation score." Arcs above the axis = years where data-science-oriented activity increased (technical work, courses, projects). Arcs below = years dominated by psychology/sales (the past trajectory). Arc magnitude encodes the degree of shift.

The inflection point (when arcs start consistently rising) is annotated with a label ("Started ML coursework," "First data project"). This is particularly effective for addressing a common recruiter concern about career-change candidates: is the transition real, or just on paper?

### CV Idea: Slope Graph — Career Attribute Rankings Across Career Stages
- **What it visualizes:** Key career attributes (technical depth, human insight, analytical rigor, client interaction) showing how their relative ranking shifted across 4 career stages
- **How it works:** Columns = career stages (Psychology degree → Sales role → Software development → Current/ML6 target). Lines = attributes. Lines connecting the same attribute across stages reveal where the trajectory bends: technical depth rises sharply; sales/client interaction stays high throughout; human insight remains a persistent differentiator.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Steep upward slopes in technical depth and data science skills make the growth narrative visually immediate. Flat lines for communication and people skills show persistent strengths. The crossing lines — where technical depth overtakes the previous dominance of client-facing work — make the career pivot the visual focal point. The structure of slope graphs is designed to make this type of rank-change story visible (the exact insight that makes bump charts compelling).

### CV Idea: Swoosh Alignment — From Social to Technical
- **What it visualizes:** The career pivot as a visual directional migration: nodes represent career events, and connecting swoosh lines shift progressively from left (social/commercial side) to right (analytical/technical side)
- **How it works:** Vertical timeline. Two "sides": left = social/commercial orientation, right = analytical/technical orientation. Each job or study milestone is a node. Connecting lines between nodes swoosh toward the technical side over time — the swoosh direction across the timeline makes the career direction change legible at a glance. No text needed for the main argument.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Psychology degree node = left-leaning swoosh; sales jobs = center to left; first software development work = moving right; current data science study = fully right. The metaphor maps to the concept of career allegiance shifting — analogous to a character in a narrative changing sides, which is how growth looks from the outside.

## Section 5 — Character Traits / Personality

### CV Idea: Metaphor-Driven Personality Glyph — Growing Plant as Career Metaphor
- **What it visualizes:** Personality traits, values, and soft skills as annotated callout labels from a central growing-plant illustration
- **How it works:** A central growing-plant illustration (thematically resonant given the data project involving actual plants). Callout lines pointing outward. Left-side callouts: soft skills and personality traits (social intelligence, adaptability, creativity, negotiation). Right-side callouts: technical/analytical traits (structured thinking, pattern recognition, curiosity, systems thinking). Language proficiency shown as small bars below the illustration. The plant metaphor: roots = foundational psychology and social skills; trunk = commercial/sales experience; branches = software and data skills currently growing; new shoots = AI/ML direction.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Directly resonant for someone who did ecology-related data work — the visual metaphor links the CV to the portfolio. The plant metaphor is semantically unusual for a data science CV, which is exactly its strength: it signals creative thinking, and the organic growth narrative matches the career trajectory. Memorable to a recruiter scanning dozens of CVs.

### CV Idea: Honeycomb / Hexagon Personality and Values Map
- **What it visualizes:** Personality traits, work style preferences, and values arranged spatially — positive traits radiating outward, with optional honest encoding of what the candidate actively avoids
- **How it works:** Central hexagon = candidate name/title. Surrounding hexagons: filled with personality traits and work style attributes. Color = trait category (social/interpersonal, analytical, creative, technical). Size = relative emphasis. Optional second ring: things the candidate actively dislikes in work environments ("I struggle with purely repetitive tasks", "I thrive when working across domains") — communicates self-awareness and honesty.
- **Adaptation for our context:** Inner ring = core strengths (social intelligence, analytical curiosity, creative problem-solving, structured communication). Outer ring = growing capabilities (machine learning, statistical modeling, data storytelling). The "I thrive when / I struggle with" extension signals emotional intelligence and self-awareness — both valued at collaborative applied-ML environments like ML6. Self-awareness from a psychology background gives genuine credibility to this section.

### CV Idea: Trait Self-Map — Participant Places Marker on Trait Axes
- **What it visualizes:** 4–6 personality/professional trait dimensions shown as horizontal scales or radial axes. A single marker (dot, sticker-style icon) placed by the candidate on each axis. Brief annotation explains each self-placement with one cited example.
- **How it works:** Each axis anchored by two endpoint descriptors (e.g., Rigorous ↔ Intuitive; Introvert ↔ Extrovert; Detail-focused ↔ Big-picture). The candidate marks their position. One-sentence annotation per axis cites a specific example (e.g., "Rigorous: built automated test coverage for sales pipeline tool").
- **Adaptation for our context:** The psychology background gives genuine credibility to self-insight claims — most people claiming "I'm highly analytical" cannot back it with a degree in the scientific study of human behavior. The sales experience supports the extroversion / communicative end of the people-skills axes. The sticker-placement metaphor signals familiarity with data visualization culture — appropriate for ML6. Keep to 4 dimensions maximum for print readability.
- **Page reference:** DS p.409

### CV Idea: What-Why-How Grid for Key Projects — Demonstrating Analytical Framing
- **What it visualizes:** 2–3 key projects, each summarized as a compact three-column block: What (data type and scale) → Why (abstract task and user need) → How (method, tool, output)
- **How it works:** Each project is one row with three cells. "What" describes the data and context. "Why" describes the abstract analytical task (discover, compare, summarize, present) and who needed it. "How" describes the specific method and tool. This structure replaces standard bullet-point job descriptions with structured micro-analyses demonstrating analytical thinking.
- **Adaptation for our context:** ML6 and applied ML teams value candidates who frame problems analytically before reaching for tools. The what-why-how structure signals cultural and intellectual fit. Describing a sales analytics project using this structure — "What: customer churn data for 200 accounts / Why: identify patterns predicting attrition (discover task) / How: behavioral pattern analysis, Excel pivot tables, resulting in segmentation model" — reframes a sales role as feature engineering experience. Mirrors the vocabulary used in data science job descriptions and academic papers.
- **Page reference:** VAD p.68–71, VAD p.84–86

## Section 6 — Layout and Overall Structure

### CV Idea: The Three-Part Story Resume — Introduction, Main Event, Call-to-Action
- **What it visualizes:** The overall narrative arc of the career, structured as three visual zones
- **How it works:** Three vertically stacked sections: (1) Introduction/Foundation (top): who this is and why they matter — Psychology degree, current data science study, a one-line career pivot statement, 2 key credentials as visual anchors. (2) Main Event (center, visually dominant): the unusual combination — psychology + software + data science. The most visually distinctive element lives here: skill radar or "dual-track" visual showing the analytical + interpersonal axes simultaneously. (3) Conclusion/Call-to-Action (bottom): contact info, LinkedIn, GitHub, portfolio link — visually prominent. Monroe's Motivated Sequence structure: (1) Key message → (2) Problem AI needs → (3) Why this background is the differentiator → (4) Proof (timeline, metrics) → (5) Contact.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The introduction establishes the breadth; the main event makes the unusual combination the selling point rather than an apology; the call-to-action is the internship ask. The persuasive structure prevents the reader from dismissing the psychology/sales background as irrelevant — it builds the case that these skills are exactly what distinguishes the candidate from a conventional CS graduate.
- **Page reference:** CI p.35–37, CI p.85–86

### CV Idea: Jack Hagley Multi-Panel — One Page Answering All Questions
- **What it visualizes:** Personality type, time allocation, skill proficiency, career history, and interests — all on one page with minimal text
- **How it works:** Multiple small panels on a single page, each answering a specific question about the person: (1) Bubble cluster or treemap = skills and relative importance; (2) Trait sliders or self-map = "how I look at the world" (4–6 dimensions); (3) Radial time-allocation chart = what the candidate does with their day (study / work / projects / learning); (4) Timeline strip = career and education history; (5) Icon row = hobbies and interests; (6) Key metrics as large numbers (years experience, projects completed, etc.). Deliberately minimal color palette — entirely printable.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The psychology background makes the personality/trait panel unusually credible — this person actually studied human behavior and can assess themselves with methodological rigor. The time allocation wheel showing current split (studying, working, projects) signals active commitment to the pivot. Works exceptionally well for applied ML companies that value personality alongside technical skills. Black-and-white version prints perfectly on any office printer.
- **Page reference:** CI p.162 (Jack Hagley design)

### CV Idea: Coordinated Multiple Views — Timeline + Radar + Project Cards
- **What it visualizes:** Three juxtaposed views of the same career data sharing a consistent color encoding: a timeline of experiences, a radar of current skill levels, a brief project list with linked highlighting
- **How it works:** All views share one color encoding (domain color: psychology = warm, sales = gold, dev = blue, ML = purple). Timeline shows chronology (main column). Radar shows current state (right sidebar). 2–3 highlighted project cards with matching color dots appear below. The shared color encoding links information across views without requiring text cross-references.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The coordinated views answer three distinct recruiter questions simultaneously: "What did they do? What can they do now? What have they built?" The radar's asymmetric shape (psychology and communication axes larger than baseline) argues for the unusual profile. For print: keep colors consistent. For digital: hover highlighting connects each domain color across all three panels. Keeps the total visualization count within the "three or fewer" rule.

### CV Idea: Overview First — Compact Heatmap Header + Detail Below
- **What it visualizes:** The full CV structured following "Overview First, Zoom and Filter, Details on Demand" — a compact visual summary at the top functions as a navigational anchor for the text below
- **How it works:** Top third of the page: a dense 4×6 skills heatmap (rows = skill categories, columns = time periods or role contexts, color saturation = proficiency) plus a 3-sentence career narrative. The heatmap immediately shows breadth and trajectory at a glance. Below: conventional text sections (work history, education, projects) that expand the overview. Each text section is spatially aligned with its corresponding heatmap column.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The heatmap at the top is itself a demonstration of data visualization competence — the medium reinforces the message. For ML6, the right-most columns (recent data science work) are the darkest, showing the recruiter exactly where investment has been concentrated. The text below provides the narrative detail. Must work in grayscale (luminance = proficiency level, hue = skill category).

### CV Idea: Area-Correct Bubble Timeline — Experience Weighted by Duration
- **What it visualizes:** Work and education history as a horizontal timeline of bubbles, where each bubble's area (correctly calculated as pi × r squared, not diameter) is proportional to the duration of that role in months
- **How it works:** Each role/degree is a bubble positioned at its start date. Bubble area proportional to duration. Bubble color = domain. Label inside or beside each bubble: role name and organization. Partially filled or dotted outline bubble at the right edge = "in progress" current data science study.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The correct area sizing (not just diameter) is itself a demonstration of data literacy — a subtle but meaningful signal to a data science recruiter. The multi-domain breadth and recency of the data science pivot are immediately readable. The in-progress visual at the right edge creates forward momentum, showing the career is not static.
- **Page reference:** CI p.203–205

### CV Idea: Focus+Context Timeline — DOI-Driven Space Allocation
- **What it visualizes:** A career timeline where the most recent and most relevant experiences receive the most visual space, while older/less directly relevant experiences are compressed but still visible
- **How it works:** The degree-of-interest (DOI) function is adapted: interest I(x) = relevance to data science / ML. High-DOI items (ML projects, data analysis, statistical research) get full detail (title, description, bullet points, tech stack). Lower-DOI items (pure sales administration, generic software work) are compressed to one-line summaries. The visual hierarchy communicates "I know what ML6 needs and I've organized this CV to answer that question."
- **Adaptation for our context:** Psychology research → DOI: high (experimental design = statistical methods); sales role → DOI: medium (stakeholder framing of insights, behavioral analytics); software dev → DOI: high (technical foundation). Layout: ML/data science projects in the main column with full detail; psychology/sales/software in a compressed sidebar. The visual compression signals intentionality — the candidate has curated, not hidden, their background.
- **Page reference:** VAD p.348–350, DS p.356–358, VAD p.323–338

### CV Idea: The 5-Second Key Message Banner — Phase 1 → Phase 2 Visual Header
- **What it visualizes:** Career positioning as a single visually dominant block at the top of the CV, communicating the core narrative before any text is read
- **How it works:** A large, visually prominent header block (not a generic objective statement in small font). Shows two phases with an arrow: Phase 1 icons (psychology, human, sales) → Phase 2 icons (data science, ML, analytical tools). Colors: warm tones for Phase 1, cool tones for Phase 2. The arrow is the pivot argument. Beneath the icons: a one-line positioning statement in bold readable at arm's length.
- **Adaptation for our context:** The key message for this CV is the pivot: human skills (psychology, sales) + developed technical competence (data science, ML). Placing this visually at the top ensures even a 6-second scan delivers the core argument. The two-phase format prevents the background from looking like a liability — it positions it as Phase 1 of a deliberate two-phase trajectory.
- **Page reference:** CI p.208

## Appendix — Additional Ideas Worth Considering

These ideas have strong adaptation logic but overlap significantly with entries above, or are best suited to digital/interactive formats rather than print CVs.

### Digital-Only Ideas
- **Scrollytelling Career Narrative** (DS p.137–143): Same career marks rearranging under scroll to show domain grouping → chronology → skill type → pivot moments. Best for a portfolio website, not a print CV.
- **Linked Highlighting CV** (VAD p.292–294): Hover a skill, all experiences where it was applied light up. Hover an experience, all skills it involved light up. Requires a web implementation.
- **Animated Transition Before/After Profile** (VAD p.248–249): Radar or bar chart animating from "entering the program" to "current state." Effective as a portfolio opener or cover letter attachment.
- **Dynamic Query Self-Presentation / FilmFinder Pattern** (VAD p.326–328): Interactive scatterplot of projects where the recruiter filters by skill category to see relevant experiences highlighted. Demonstrates interaction design skills — best as a Tableau/Observable portfolio piece.

### Metaphor-Based Decorative Graphics (Print Accent)
- **Climate Stripes** (EC p.2): A horizontal strip of colored columns where each column = one month/quarter of the career and color encodes data science learning intensity (dark = intensive). Striking as a header bar accent. No legend needed.
- **Beads-on-a-String Timeline** (DS p.282–295): Each role as a bead on a winding string with a mini radar glyph inside each bead. Visually distinctive but complex — best for a portfolio page rather than a one-page print CV.
- **Skill Fractal Flows** (DS p.78–88): Three career domains as colored flowing fractal lines that converge toward the present. Primarily decorative/emotional — best as a full-page hero graphic or cover image.

### Honest Self-Assessment Techniques
- **Threat-to-Validity Risk Table** (VAD p.99–100): Two-column table — "Potential recruiter concern" → "How I address it." Semantically novel: applies a visualization theory framework to self-presentation. Proactively answers objections. Best in a cover letter rather than the CV itself.
- **Contextual Comparison / Diverging Baseline** (CI p.29–31): Show each skill in comparison to "typical psychology graduate" (grey) vs. "my profile" (colored). Makes unusual strengths visible as above-baseline rather than as isolated bars.
